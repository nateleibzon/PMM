01

Messaging: rewriting the Renters page hero

Skill demonstrated — Messaging & copywriting

Lemonade's renters product leads with price and speed: a policy from $5/month, a quote in 90 seconds, and roughly 40% of claims paid instantly. Those are strong, true proof points. But the current hero treats them as the headline — and price-led headlines invite a price comparison the customer then loses, because a renter who owns a car often finds a bundled State Farm policy cheaper once the multi-line discount is applied.

The fix isn't to drop the $5. It's to lead with the feeling renters actually want — protection without the dread of dealing with an insurer — and let price land as a reason to believe, not the whole pitch.

Before — current direction

Renters insurance from $5/month.

Get covered in 90 seconds. Cancel anytime. Powered by AI.

After — proposed rewrite

The stuff you love, covered before your coffee's cold.

Renters insurance that takes 90 seconds to start and pays most claims in minutes — from $5/month. No agents, no hold music, no catch.

Why this wins The rewrite keeps every proof point but reorders the logic: outcome → ease → price. "Before your coffee's cold" is concrete and ownable; "no hold music" names the incumbent pain without naming a competitor. Price moves to the supporting line, so the customer evaluates Lemonade on experience — the dimension Lemonade actually wins — instead of on a bundled-rate math problem it can't always win.

Three supporting messages, by funnel stage

  • Awareness — "Renting? Your landlord's insurance doesn't cover your stuff. Yours should." Reframes the category for first-time renters who don't know they need it.
  • Consideration — "See what's covered before you pay a cent." Speaks to the quote flow as a no-risk way to learn, lowering the cost of starting.
  • Decision — "Switch in the time it takes to read this sentence." Removes the final friction — the assumption that changing insurers is a chore.

Positioning: the canvas, and the fight Lemonade is quietly losing

Skill demonstrated — Positioning & competitive strategy

Here is a positioning canvas for Lemonade Renters as I'd fill it in today.

Target
Digitally-native renters, ~22–34, in their first or second apartment, who view insurance as a chore to dispatch — not a relationship to maintain.
Frame of reference
Renters insurance — but the real competitive set is "the friction of getting covered at all," including doing nothing.
Point of difference
A genuinely AI-native experience — quote in 90 seconds, instant claims, no agents — built that way from day one, not retrofitted onto legacy systems.
Reason to believe
3M+ customers; ~40% of claims paid instantly; the Giveback model; pricing from $5/month.
Statement
For renters who want protection without the hassle, Lemonade is the insurance built for software speed — covering you in seconds and paying claims in minutes — because it was designed AI-first, while incumbents bolt AI onto century-old systems.

The competitor out-messaging Lemonade — and it isn't another insurtech

It's State Farm. Not on brand or experience — Lemonade wins those easily — but on the one question a renter-with-a-car actually asks: "what will I pay?"

State Farm's multi-line bundle discount lets it advertise a higher renters rate and still deliver a lower total bill once auto is attached. Lemonade advertises "$5/month," the renter does the math with their car included, and the apparent value flips. Independent 2026 reviews repeatedly land on the same verdict: cheaper on renters alone, but if you own a car, bundling usually wins. Lemonade's headline sets up a comparison its pricing structure can't reliably win.

Lemonade's positioning gap isn't awareness — it's that the headline picks a price fight on the incumbent's home turf. Win the experience argument loudly enough that price stops being the deciding question.

The recommendation Shift the comparative narrative from "we're cheaper" to "we're the only one that actually feels like 2026." Reserve price for renters without a car (where Lemonade genuinely wins outright), and for everyone else, compete on speed, transparency, and the claims experience — then neutralize the bundle directly with Lemonade Car cross-sell (see Section 3).

Go-to-market: launching "Bundle & Save" for renters who drive

Skill demonstrated — Go-to-market strategy

Sections 1 and 2 surfaced one structural weakness: the renter-with-a-car defaults to a bundled incumbent. Lemonade already sells Car insurance and a renters product across most of the US — the pieces exist. What's missing is a deliberate renters → car bundle motion that meets State Farm's discount head-on. Here's the GTM plan for it.

Launch one-liner

"Already a Lemonade renter? Add your car and watch both bills drop — in under two minutes, no phone call required."

Objective & success metric

  • Primary: lift renters-to-car attach rate among eligible existing renters in launch states.
  • Guardrail: no degradation in renters retention or blended loss ratio.
  • North star for the launch: share of new car policies originating from the existing renters base.
Phase 1 — FoundationsWeeks 0–3

Lock the eligible audience (renters in states where Lemonade Car is live). Build the in-app "add your car" path off an existing policy so the quote pre-fills known data. Set the bundle discount and validate unit economics with actuarial + finance.

Exit criteria — bundle quote flow live in 3+ states, discount approved.

Phase 2 — Soft launchWeeks 4–7

Lifecycle-only: target renters at natural trigger moments — renewal, an address change, app re-engagement. Channels: in-app banner, push, email. No paid spend yet. A/B test two messages: "save on both" vs. "one app for everything."

Exit criteria — attach-rate signal beats control; winning message identified.

Phase 3 — Scale & acquireWeeks 8–14

Roll the winning message to all eligible renters. Flip the funnel for acquisition: run paid campaigns positioning Lemonade as the bundle built for renters first — directly countering the incumbent's auto-first bundle. Brief support on the new flow.

Exit criteria — bundle is a measurable share of new car policies; CAC within target.

Risks & how I'd manage them

  • Loss-ratio risk — car insurance carries different risk; gate the discount on underwriting, not marketing. Finance holds veto.
  • Cannibalization — a discount to renters who'd have bought car anyway. Mitigate by measuring against a held-out control, not gross numbers.
  • State coverage gaps — messaging must be geo-aware so renters in non-Car states never see an offer they can't take.

Market research: the segment Lemonade is under-indexing on

Skill demonstrated — Market research & segmentation

The one-page argument: graduating renters

Lemonade has built a remarkable engine for acquiring young renters. The under-indexed opportunity isn't a new audience — it's a lifecycle transition inside the one Lemonade already owns: the renter who becomes a first-time homebuyer, or who moves in with a partner, gets a pet, or buys a car.

This matters because of a structural fact about insurtech economics: renters policies are low-premium. The customer is acquired cheaply and loved early — but the lifetime value only compounds if Lemonade captures the next policy when that renter's life gets more complex. Lemonade already sells home, pet, car, and life. The gap is not product. It's a deliberate lifecycle-marketing motion that detects the trigger moment and moves the customer up the value ladder before an incumbent does.

Why the window is now

  • The cohort is aging into it. Renters acquired in Lemonade's early growth years are now hitting first-home, first-pet, first-car life stages — the exact moments insurance decisions get made.
  • Incumbents win by default at these moments. A mortgage lender hands the buyer a home-insurance quote at closing. If Lemonade isn't present at the trigger, it loses a customer it already paid to acquire.
  • The AutonomousCar / FSD launch proves Lemonade can move fast on new lines — the muscle exists; it needs to be pointed at its own base.

What I'd recommend investigating first

  • Quantify it: what share of churned renters bought a competing home or car policy within 12 months of leaving?
  • Map the trigger signals Lemonade can already see — address changes, app behavior, life-event survey data.
  • Size the prize: incremental LTV of moving even a modest share of renters to a second policy vs. the cost of the lifecycle program.

This is a one-page thesis, not a finished study — but it names a specific, defensible segment and the exact data needed to validate it, which is what a research one-pager is for.

Persona: one sharp profile, not three shallow ones

Skill demonstrated — Persona development

M

"Lease-Deadline Maya"

27 · first solo apartment · marketing coordinator · renter, no car

Context

Just signed her first lease without a roommate. Her landlord's addendum requires proof of renters insurance before move-in. She has four days.

Goal

Tick the box, fast, without a phone call — and ideally feel like she made a smart-adult decision, not just a forced one.

Pains & anxieties

Doesn't know how much coverage is "right." Suspicious of fine print. Assumes insurance means hold music and a hard sell.

What wins her

A quote she can finish on her phone in one sitting, plain-language coverage, and a price that feels fair for someone furnishing a flat on a budget.

"I don't want to 'talk to an agent.' I want to handle this tonight and never think about it again — unless something actually happens."

Why this persona, and only this one: she is the renter Lemonade wins outright — no car, so no bundle math, no incumbent advantage. She is also lease-deadline motivated, which makes her the cleanest test audience for the urgency and guidance experiments in Section 7. A PMM who tries to serve "renters, homeowners, and pet owners" all at once writes copy that's true for everyone and sharp for no one. Maya is specific enough to write a single headline against — and most of this teardown is written against her.

How I'd use her Maya becomes the protagonist of the messaging in Section 1, the entry point of the journey in Section 4, and the target cohort for the experiment in Section 7. One persona, used consistently across every deliverable — that's the difference between a persona that ships work and a persona that sits in a slide.

Growth: one metric, one experiment, one prediction

Skill demonstrated — Conversion, activation & retention

I won't propose ten experiments. A good PMM picks the highest-leverage one and commits to a falsifiable prediction. Here it is.

The metric

Quote-start → quote-complete conversion rate, for renters in the coverage-selection step (Section 4, step 3).

Chosen because it's mid-funnel — high traffic already there, and the friction is a known UX problem, not a demand problem.

The hypothesis

First-time renters abandon at coverage selection because they don't know what's "normal." If we anchor the choice with social proof, decision paralysis drops and completion rises.

The experiment

A/B test in the renters quote flow. Control: current coverage sliders. Variant: the same sliders, plus a smart default and an anchor line — "Renters in your area typically choose around $30k of personal property coverage" — with a one-tap "use this" option. Audience: first-time renters ("Lease-Deadline Maya"). Primary metric: step-3 completion. Guardrails: no drop in policies bound, no rise in under-insurance complaints.

The prediction

+8–12%relative lift in step-3 completion

Anchoring effects on a paralysis-prone choice are well-documented; a defaulted, socially-proofed option reliably moves completion in this range.

If I'm wrong

If completion doesn't move, the constraint isn't paralysis — it's price or intent. That's still a win: it redirects effort from UX to the pricing message in Section 1. A clean negative result is a real result.

The reason this experiment is the right one: it sits exactly where Sections 4 and 6 already pointed — the same friction step, the same persona — so it isn't a random test, it's the logical conclusion of the whole teardown. That coherence, deciding what not to do and connecting one insight to the next, is the actual job.
